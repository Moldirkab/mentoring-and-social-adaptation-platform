1. Summary
The evidence shows a clear, documented need in Kazakhstan and internationally:

The group is large and still exists despite deinstitutionalisation. Kazakhstan has about 20,800 orphans and children left without parental care (2026), and about 4,000 still live in residential institutions, which they leave at 18 [1]–[3].
Leaving care is followed by worse outcomes in every life domain. A systematic review of 32 studies found that care leavers lag behind peers in education, employment, income, housing, health and criminal involvement [4]. In England 40% of care leavers aged 19–21 are not in education, employment or training, against about 15% of all young people [5].
Housing and independent living are the most acute problems in Kazakhstan. 62,593 orphans were in the state housing queue at the end of 2023, and some regions allocated no more than 13% of housing to them instead of the required 20% [6]. In a 2026 Kazakh study, 42% of graduates often had housing difficulties and 71% had never sought help [7].
Mental-health risk is high and long-lasting. A Swedish national cohort found that, after adjustment, care leavers had 3.4–4.2 times the suicide risk of the general population, still elevated from age 18 to 48 [8].
Mentoring is a promising, state-recognised response, but it is small, stops at 18 and is administered on paper. Kazakhstan created a formal mentoring institute for orphans in 2024 [9]; more than 360 mentors work in 20 regions [1]. The rules require monthly meeting reports and quarterly organisational reports, and mentoring ends on the child's 18th birthday [9]. A meta-analysis shows a small-to-medium effect of mentoring for youth in care [10], and a systematic review names a steady mentor after leaving care as a protective factor [4].
Together these findings justify a platform that helps organisations recruit and screen mentors, form pairs, keep contact safe and visible, keep the records the rules already require, and continue support after 18.

2. Context: how many young people are affected
Total population. The Ministry of Education reports 20,798 orphans and children without parental care in January 2026, 82% of them in families [1]. Bureau of National Statistics data for 2023, as cited by CABAR, give 21,387 in total, of whom 3,727 (17.4%) lived in institutions for orphans [3].
Institutions are shrinking but not gone. The number of children in orphanages fell from about 9,000 to 4,000 over ten years (Vice-Minister of Education E. Ospan, March 2025) [2]. The Ministry reports a 24% smaller network of institutions and 54% fewer pupils over ten years [1].
Why it matters for the platform: every year a cohort of young people leaves institutions at 18, and several thousand children are still in residential care. The target group is real and ongoing, not historical.

3. Key findings by theme
3.1 Social adaptation and independent living
In a 2026 study of orphanage graduates in Kazakhstan (at least 100 graduates, mostly in Almaty, plus expert interviews and focus groups), 41.5% often faced material hardship, 42% often had housing difficulties, 16.5% often had financial-literacy problems, and 71% had never sought assistance, mainly because of personal reluctance (42.3%) or lack of trust that help would work (34.5%) [7].
Experts in the same study estimated that only about 10% of graduates achieve stable integration into education, work and family life [7]. This is an expert estimate, not a measured rate; the 71% figure is stated in the text but not shown in the article's tables, and the authors say the study does not aim for statistical generalisation.
3.2 Housing
The Commissioner for Children's Rights reported in December 2023 that about 645,000 citizens were waiting for housing, including 62,593 orphans, and that some akimats did not meet the 20% quota for orphans, allocating no more than 13% [6]. The source does not say whether the 62,593 includes adults who grew up as orphans.
For comparison, 13,068 rental apartments were issued to all eligible categories of citizens in 2024 [2].
Internationally, housing is one of the domains where care leavers consistently lag behind peers [4].
3.3 Education
In England in 2025, only 6% of care leavers aged 19–21 were in higher education and 21% in other education [5].
Education is one of the domains where care leavers lag behind peers in both child-protection and family-service systems [4].
No national data on Kazakh graduates' educational outcomes were found (gap).
3.4 Employment
In the 2026 Kazakh study, 15.5% of graduates often had difficulty securing stable employment and 41.5% often faced material hardship [7].
In England, 40% of care leavers aged 19–21 were NEET in 2025, against an estimated 15% of all 19–21-year-olds [5].
No national employment statistics for Kazakh graduates were found (gap).
3.5 Mental health
In a Swedish national cohort, adults with a history of out-of-home care had, after full adjustment, 3.39 (men) and 4.23 (women) times the risk of suicide and 4.47 (men) and 4.25 (women) times the risk of hospitalised self-harm compared with the general population; the risk stayed similarly high from age 18 to 48 [8].
In Kazakhstan, 11.5% of graduates in the 2026 study often had psychological problems, and most never sought help [7].
3.6 Mentoring and support
Effect. A multilevel meta-analysis of nine US programmes for youth in foster care found a small-to-medium effect of mentoring (g = 0.34); near-peer mentors were more effective than older mentors [10]. A systematic review names a steady mentor who supports youth after they age out of care among the factors linked to better outcomes [4].
Kazakhstan's current model. Order No. 97 of the Minister of Education (30 April 2024, new edition 30 April 2025) sets the rules for mentoring orphans [9]:
mentors are screened (criminal record, health, income, home visit) and must hold a training certificate;
mentoring covers children in care organisations from age 10 and ends when the child turns 18;
mentors keep a meeting diary and submit monthly reports by the 10th of the next month; mentoring organisations submit quarterly reports to the guardianship authority;
mentors must report threats to the child's life or health and keep the child's information confidential.
Scale. More than 360 mentors work in 20 regions (January 2026) [1]. Against roughly 3,700–4,000 children in institutions [2], [3], this is fewer than one mentor per ten children, assuming one child per mentor (our rough calculation).

5. Conclusion: what the evidence means for the platform
Documented problem	Evidence	Platform response
Graduates lack a trusted adult and rarely ask for help	[7], [4]	Mentor matching and a low-threshold chat with a known mentor
Mentoring ends at 18, exactly when housing and work problems start	[9], [6], [7]	Pairs can continue after 18 as graduate mentoring; the pre-18 relationship history carries over
Mentoring is scarce (≈360 mentors for ~4,000 children in institutions)	[1]–[3]	Online application, screening checklist and matching so coordinators can handle more pairs
Rules require screening, monthly meeting reports and quarterly organisational reports	[9]	Document checklist for mentor screening, meeting diary, generated monthly and quarterly reports
Child safety and confidentiality obligations	[9]	Communication inside the platform, visible to the coordinator, with incident reporting
Needs are practical: housing, money, work, mental health	[5]–[8]	Skill tracks (housing rights and the queue, budgeting, job search, where to get psychological help)
Mentoring works best when relationships last	[4], [10]	Last-contact tracking and inactivity alerts so pairs do not fade out unnoticed
Limits of this evidence. Kazakh outcome data come from one non-random study [7]; there are no national statistics on graduates' employment, education or mental health. Several Kazakh figures come from officials quoted in media [1], [2], [6] rather than from published statistical tables. International data [4], [5], [8], [10] describe systems that differ from Kazakhstan's. This is why local interviews are needed.
